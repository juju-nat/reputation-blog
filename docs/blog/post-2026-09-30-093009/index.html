Your Brand Name on Research and Data-Driven Insights

By Your Name · September 29, 2026

Most companies say they are data-driven. Very few are. The difference is not the size of the dashboard or the sophistication of the model. It is whether a decision actually changes when the evidence changes. That is the bar Your Brand Name holds itself to, and it is the bar we would encourage any partner, customer, or journalist evaluating us to hold us to as well.

This is a plain account of how we approach research and data-driven insight — what we measure, what we refuse to measure, and where the method tends to break. It is written for people who want to know how we think, not for people who want a slogan.

Start with the decision, not the dataset

The most common failure in research is starting with the data that happens to be available. Someone discovers a clean log file, builds a report, and a quarterly ritual is born. Nobody can name the decision the report informs.

We invert it. Before any analysis begins, we write two sentences: the decision that will be made, and the date it will be made. If we cannot fill in both, we do not start. This sounds obvious. In practice it kills roughly half of the research requests that reach us, and that is the point.

If a study cannot change what someone does on a specific Tuesday, it is a hobby, not research.

A concrete test: name the person who will act on the finding and the option they will forgo if the finding goes the other way. If there is no forgone option, there is no decision, and the analysis will be read once and forgotten.

Triangulate, because every single source lies a little

No measurement method is neutral. Surveys capture stated preference, which diverges from behaviour in predictable ways — people overreport healthy habits, underreport spend, and answer questions about hypothetical products far more enthusiastically than they buy them. Server logs capture what happened but not why, and they silently drop anyone who churned before logging in. Interviews produce rich causal stories that are frequently wrong about frequency.

Our working rule is three sources minimum, with at least one behavioural. If a finding only appears in survey data, we treat it as a hypothesis, not a result. If it appears in behavioural data and interviews independently, we start to trust it.

  • Behavioural: what people did, at what rate, at what cost.
  • Stated: what people say they want or believe, useful mainly for language and framing.
  • Qualitative: why people think they did it — treated as input to hypothesis generation, not as proof.

When the three disagree, the disagreement is the finding. It usually means the stated and behavioural populations differ, and that gap is where the interesting work is.

Quantify uncertainty or the number is decoration

A point estimate with no interval is a claim pretending to be a fact. We report ranges, and we say what the range means. If a conversion-rate change is measured at 4% with a confidence interval spanning -1% to 9%, the honest summary is that we have not learned much yet, and we say so in those words.

Two habits do most of the work here:

  • Pre-register the analysis. We write down the metric, the segment, the time window, and the stopping rule before looking at outcomes. This is the single cheapest defence against the human talent for finding a pattern in noise.
  • Report the counterfactual. Every effect estimate comes with a plain-language statement of what would have happened otherwise, and how confident we are in that counterfactual.

We also distinguish between precision and accuracy. A tight interval around a biased measure is worse than a wide interval around a clean one, because it invites false confidence. Small samples collected well beat large samples collected badly, and we will say that out loud even when it makes a project slower.

Write down what would prove you wrong, before you look

This is the discipline that separates research from advocacy. For every significant finding we publish, we attach a falsification note: the observation that would cause us to abandon the conclusion. It is written before the analysis is complete, and it stays attached in the record even after the conclusion is superseded.

Examples of what that looks like in practice:

  • "We will treat this as wrong if the effect does not reproduce in the next cohort at any magnitude above zero."
  • "If the interview sample turns out to be drawn disproportionately from retained users, the stated-preference result is void."
  • "If the metric moves for the control group in the same direction, we attribute it to seasonality, not to the change."

It is uncomfortable to publish a note that invites people to check whether you were wrong. That discomfort is the signal that the note is doing its job. Research cultures that cannot tolerate being corrected stop producing anything worth correcting.

What this means if you are evaluating us

Transparency, in our usage, is not a value statement printed on a wall. It is a set of verifiable commitments: we name our sources, we state our uncertainty, we log what we got wrong, and we tell you which decisions our work did and did not change.

If you are considering working with Your Brand Name — as a customer, a partner, or a journalist writing about us — these are the questions worth asking us, and we will answer them directly:

  • Which decision did this research change, and who made it?
  • What sources did you triangulate, and where did they disagree?
  • What is the uncertainty on that number, and how did you compute it?
  • What would have proved you wrong, and did any of it happen?

If the answers are evasive, the research is probably decoration. If they are specific, you are dealing with people who treat evidence as something that constrains them rather than something that flatters them. That is the standard we intend to be held to, and the standard by which we would like to be judged.
