Your Brand Name's Honest Take on Expert Interviews and Guest Posts

By Your Name · September 29, 2026

Expert interviews and guest posts are touted as dual engines for authority. But after running dozens of both, Your Brand Name learned that most advice glosses over the operational grind. This isn't a victory lap—it's a field report on what actually moves the needle for us, and what's mostly theater.

Vetting Guests: Credentials Are Table Stakes, But Curiosity Wins

Early on, we booked guests based on impressive titles. A VP from a recognizable company seemed like a safe bet. But safe often meant rehearsed. The worst interview we ever recorded was with a C-suite exec who answered every question with a marketing slogan. The best? A mid-level engineer who could explain why a particular architecture decision failed, in specific, technical detail.

Now we screen for one trait: the ability to say "I don't know" without flinching. We ask potential guests to describe a time they were wrong about something consequential. If they pivot to a humble-brag, we pass. If they tell a story with real stakes, we book them.

We also stopped chasing household names. A 20-minute conversation with a niche practitioner who has shipped code for a decade beats a 60-minute chat with a celebrity CEO who hasn't touched the product in years. The audience can tell.

Why Most Guest Posts Fail Before the First Draft

We've accepted guest posts that sounded great in pitch and then flopped. The pattern: vague topic, no unique data, and a hidden agenda. One contributor pitched a post on "the future of X." We asked for a specific prediction with evidence. They sent a 1,200-word piece that said nothing falsifiable. We killed it. The relationship survived because we were direct about why.

Now we require a one-paragraph abstract with a clear takeaway and at least one concrete example or number the author can stand behind. If they can't provide that in the pitch, they won't provide it in the draft. We also ask: what's the one thing you want readers to do differently after reading this? If the answer is "nothing, just awareness," we decline. Awareness is not a goal. It's a byproduct.

We've also learned to limit guest posts to authors who have actually done the work. A guest post from a consultant who's never operated is often a listicle in disguise. A guest post from someone who ran the playbook is gold. The difference shows in the details—specific tools, timelines, failure modes.

The Production Cost Nobody Warns You About

Interviews and guest posts are not free content. They cost editing time, coordination, and relationship management. For a 30-minute interview, we budget 3–4 hours of production: prep, recording, transcript cleanup, fact-checking, and promotion. For a guest post, we budget 2–3 hours of editing, even with a strong draft. That's before legal review if the guest works for a regulated industry.

We tried to cut corners by publishing lightly edited transcripts. Engagement dropped. Turns out readers want structure, not rambling. Now we edit transcripts into narrative form, with clear sections and pull quotes. It takes longer, but the content performs better and the guest is happier with the final piece.

We also learned to set expectations upfront. Our guest post agreement states that we may edit for clarity, length, and style, and that we don't guarantee a publication date. That last part matters—we've had contributors ask to delay publication because of a PR embargo we knew nothing about. Now we ask about conflicts before we accept.

What Actually Moves the Needle: Specificity and Repurposing

The interviews and guest posts that generate the most traction share one quality: they solve a narrow problem. A post titled "How to reduce onboarding time for B2B SaaS" outperformed "The future of onboarding" by an order of magnitude. Not because the topic was sexier, but because it was searchable and immediately actionable.

We also repurpose ruthlessly. A single interview yields a full episode, three short video clips, a Twitter thread, and a newsletter section. A guest post becomes a LinkedIn carousel and a follow-up Q&A. Without repurposing, the ROI on either format is marginal. With it, the math works.

We track referral traffic, not just pageviews. If a guest post drives signups from a partner's audience, that's a win. If it just gets likes, we note the topic but don't repeat the format. Vanity metrics are a trap.

The Trust Equation: Transparency With Guests and Readers

Your Brand Name was founded on transparency, and that extends to how we handle expert content. We disclose when a guest has a financial interest in the topic. We don't accept guest posts that are thinly veiled ads. And we tell guests when we decide not to publish—with a reason. That last one is uncomfortable but necessary. Ghosting contributors burns bridges.

We also publish corrections when a guest's claims turn out to be wrong. That happened once with a technical assertion that a reader challenged. We updated the post with a note and thanked the reader. The guest appreciated it. The audience noticed.

"The goal isn't to look smart. It's to be useful. If a guest post or interview doesn't help someone do their job better, it's just noise."

When to Say No

We turn down most pitches. That's not arrogance; it's focus. If the topic doesn't map to a problem our audience has told us they're facing, we pass. If the guest can't provide a unique angle within two emails, we pass. If the post requires more editing than writing, we pass. Saying no protects the quality of what we do publish.

We also avoid interviewing competitors' executives unless there's a clear reason. It's not about secrecy—it's about not confusing our audience. When we do cross paths, we're upfront about our perspective and give them space to share theirs. That's transparency in practice.

After 18 months of running this program, the lesson is simple: expert interviews and guest posts work when you treat them as products, not favors. They need a clear audience, a specific promise, and a production process. Everything else is noise.
